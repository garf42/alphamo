## Research summary

American International Group (AIG) was a multi-line global insurance + financial-products
architecture combining: (a) US commercial property-casualty insurance, (b) global life
insurance + retirement (AIG Life + SunAmerica + ALICO), (c) international general
insurance (operations in 130+ countries), (d) AIG Financial Products (AIGFP) — a non-
insurance derivatives + structured-finance + credit-default-swap subsidiary based in London
+ Wilton CT [E].

Cornelius Vander Starr founded the company 1919 in Shanghai as American Asiatic Underwriters.
Maurice "Hank" Greenberg led the company 1968-2005 — 37-year tenure during which AIG grew
from ~$300M revenue to ~$100B+ revenue + ~$1T balance sheet via aggressive multi-line +
international expansion + AIGFP-derivatives growth [E]. Greenberg departed March 2005
amid Eliot Spitzer + SEC accounting-restatement investigation; Martin Sullivan succeeded,
then Robert Willumstad June 2008 [E].

The architecture's critical 2008 failure: AIGFP wrote approximately $440B in credit-default-
swap (CDS) protection on collateralized-debt-obligations (CDOs) referencing US subprime
mortgages + other structured-credit. AIGFP's CDS positions were not capitalized as insurance
contracts (despite being economic-insurance) due to regulatory-arbitrage between insurance
regulators + securities/derivatives regulators. When subprime-mortgage defaults exceeded
expectations 2007-2008, AIGFP faced ~$60B in collateral-calls + mark-to-market losses + would
have triggered counterparty-default cascade across major banks (Goldman, Société Générale,
Merrill, Deutsche Bank, Barclays were largest CDS counterparties) [E].

September 16, 2008 — one day after Lehman Brothers bankruptcy — the Federal Reserve provided
an initial $85B credit facility to AIG in exchange for ~79.9% equity stake (via warrants).
This was extended + restructured multiple times across 2008-2009 with total US government
commitment reaching ~$182B (Fed + Treasury combined facilities + TARP) at peak. AIG paid CDS
counterparties at par on $62B of contracts using government funds — controversial
"backdoor bailout" of major banks [E].

2008-2013 restructuring: AIG sold ALICO (Asian life insurance) to MetLife $15.5B 2010 + AIA
Group Asian life IPO $20.5B 2010 + AIG Star + Edison Life + various other non-core
businesses; AIGFP wound down (gradually unwinding derivative positions); core US-property-
casualty + US-life-retirement retained. December 2012 — US Treasury fully exited AIG equity
position after receiving ~$22.7B profit on rescue (~$182B committed; ~$205B returned) [E].

Architecturally, AIG-2008 is in the **ambiguous A-vs-C zone** between Sub-pattern A
(forcibly-restructured-with-successor-entities) and Sub-pattern C (restructured-but-
preserved). Arguments for A: ~$62B+ in asset-sales-of-major-segments (ALICO + AIA + others);
US government forced restructuring via 79.9% equity stake + management control; multiple
successor entities emerged from divestments (AIA Group operating independently in Asia;
some buyer-absorbed segments). Arguments for C: AIG-the-corporate-entity preserved + still
operates as focused property-casualty insurer with retirement adjacency; core US
operations + global brand preserved; AIGFP was wound down internally not separated.
Architecture-determinant: federal-bailout-as-restructuring is novel mechanism distinct from
DOJ-antitrust-restructuring (Standard Oil + AT&T) + market-driven-restructuring (Lloyd's
1996 + Nielsen 2022).

**My classification: Sub-pattern A (forcible variant via federal-rescue-as-mechanism) +
Sub-pattern C (preserved core)** — this is the first corpus instance of **simultaneous
Sub-pattern A + Sub-pattern C** application at different segment levels of same
architecture. AIA Group + ALICO + various non-core were Sub-pattern A (forcibly-divested
as successor entities); US P&C + US Life retained were Sub-pattern C (restructured-but-
preserved within reconstituted-AIG-corporate-vehicle). This is a structurally novel
classification scenario warranting surface.
